# Working remotely from Georgia — tax, visa, work permit

Many foreigners live in Georgia and work remotely for a foreign employer or foreign clients. Staying is simple — tax is where most people go wrong: pay for work you do while physically in Georgia is Georgian income, even if your employer and your bank are abroad. Georgia has no separate digital nomad visa today. Citizens of the countries on the Government's list (Resolution No. 255 of 2015) can stay visa-free for a full year; others need a visa or a residence permit. If you plan to stay longer or register as a sole trader, plan your residence permit in advance.
Under Article 104 of the Tax Code, income from employment carried out in Georgia is Georgian-source income, and where the money is received does not matter. A foreign employer does not withhold Georgian tax (Article 154), so you must file an annual return yourself by 31 March of the following year and pay 20% (Articles 81 and 153). The exemption for foreign income (Article 82) doesn't help here — it covers only income that is not from a Georgian source.

## Do you need a work permit?

The Labour Migration Law does not apply when a foreigner works for or provides services to a non-resident and the work relates to that person's business outside Georgia (Article 1(4)(l)). So a remote employee of a foreign company usually doesn't need the right to work. A sole trader with clients in Georgia does need it since 1 March 2026; whether a sole trader serving only foreign clients falls under the exception should be checked case by case.

## The 1% sole trader — what to watch for

- The 1% applies to business income, not salary (Article 90). If you work for one company with fixed hours and under its direction, the Revenue Service may treat it as salary and tax it at 20%

- Consulting, legal, medical, audit, architecture and some other activities can't use the 1% (Government Resolution No. 415)

- Services to foreign businesses are generally outside Georgian VAT — the place of supply is where the client is (Article 162¹)

- Registering as a sole trader gives you neither a residence permit nor tax residency

## Residency and your home country

You become a Georgian tax resident if you spend 183 days or more in Georgia in any 12-month period. Your home country may still treat you as its resident and tax your worldwide income. Where a double tax treaty exists, it decides who taxes what, and a Georgian residence certificate is the proof.

## If you do nothing

- Late return — a penalty of 5% of the tax, 10% if more than 2 months late (Article 274)

- Late payment — interest of 0.05% per day

- Understated tax — a 10% to 50% penalty; large amounts can lead to a criminal case

## Questions & answers

### Is there a digital nomad visa in Georgia?

Not a separate one today. Citizens of many countries can stay visa-free for a year; for a longer stay you need a residence permit.

### Can I work remotely for a foreign employer while visa-free?

Yes. The Labour Migration Law usually doesn't require the right to work when the work relates to a foreign company's business abroad. Tax, however, is due in Georgia.

### Do digital nomads pay tax in Georgia?

Yes, for work done while in Georgia: 20% through an annual return, or 1% as a sole trader where the activity qualifies. A non-resident who works here 30 days or less a year doesn't pay tax on the salary.

### Can a foreign salary go through a 1% sole trader?

No. Salary is excluded from the 1%. If you genuinely provide services rather than work as an employee, the contract must show it — we review the contract.

### Will I still owe tax in my home country?

Possibly. That depends on your home country's residency rules and on its tax treaty with Georgia. We check both sides.
